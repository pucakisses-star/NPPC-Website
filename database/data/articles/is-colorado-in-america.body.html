In 1904 the Western Federation of Miners printed a poster in the shape of the American flag and asked a question across the top of it: Is Colorado in America?

Each stripe carried a grievance:

  • Martial law declared in Colorado!
  • Habeas corpus suspended in Colorado!
  • Free press throttled in Colorado!
  • Bull-pens for union men in Colorado!
  • Free speech denied in Colorado!
  • Soldiers defy the courts in Colorado!
  • Wholesale arrests without warrant in Colorado!
  • Union men exiled from homes and families in Colorado!
  • Constitutional right to bear arms questioned in Colorado!
  • Corporations corrupt and control administration in Colorado!
  • Right of fair, impartial and speedy trial abolished in Colorado!
  • Citizens’ alliance resorts to mob law and violence in Colorado!
  • Militia hired to corporations to break the strike in Colorado!

One version of the poster carries a photograph of Henry Maki, a striking miner arrested for “vagrancy” and chained to a telegraph pole in freezing weather when he refused to work.

The Colorado Labor Wars

The poster came out of the Colorado Labor Wars of 1903 and 1904, after nearly a decade of violent confrontation between the militant Western Federation of Miners and the mine owners and their allies in government and business. In Colorado’s War on Militant Unionism, the historian George Suggs finds plenty of violence on both sides, but argues that the owners’ refusal to negotiate at all is what fed the radicalism of the unions.

Governor James Peabody came to power as an open representative of business interests, who feared that unrest in the mining camps was deterring Eastern investment and endangering the economy of the whole state. He declared martial law and sent the National Guard to suppress the unions. He considered himself a defender of law and order, but Suggs gives repeated examples of Peabody declining to use the Guard against violence directed at union members. The power of the state ran one way — and the owners had more say still, because they financed the deployment.

Striking workers were routinely “deported” from their own communities. Some were formally declared vagrants for not working, then met with mob violence if they tried to come home. Union members were arrested en masse without charge. Where unions were strong enough to have elected their own members to office, those officials were turned out, and the Guard did not defend them.

The confrontation escalated when the federation called a sympathy strike of miners in support of smelter workers striking for an eight-hour day. Many of the miners already had the eight-hour day and a three-dollar minimum; the point was to starve the smelters of ore and break the employers’ power. Peabody and business interests across the state read it as a threat to order itself.

Arrested for the flag

It was under those conditions that federation president Charles Moyer and secretary-treasurer William “Big Bill” Haywood — later a founder of the Industrial Workers of the World — produced the flag posters. A warrant was issued for Moyer’s arrest on a charge of flag desecration.

The campaign for that arrest began with a Denver newspaperwoman, Nell Anthony, who wrote as Polly Pry. Born on a Mississippi plantation, she made her living in sensational journalism, was dismissed from the Denver Post after refusing to retract a story accusing a union leader of advocating murder, and founded her own paper — which began as a gossip sheet and became almost entirely an anti-union one. She pressed Denver’s patriotic societies to bring a desecration complaint, only to find Colorado’s law too weak: using the flag in advertising was legal.

Her counterpart on the union side was another woman journalist of Southern origin, Emma Langdon of the mining town of Victor. After the Victor Record published a story critical of the National Guard, its printing staff — including Langdon’s husband — were arrested and held without charge. Langdon went to the office at night, set and printed the paper alone, and delivered it. She was an experienced journeyman printer and an active union member in her own right, though the press of the day played up the loyal-wife angle.

Langdon asked which mattered more, the flag or what it was supposed to stand for:

“Is Colorado in America? If you consult the map you will find it there. If you read the facts in this recent industrial struggle in the light of American history and traditions, you will find nothing to recall the memories of our country’s youth. … Desecration of the flag? Was it not the deeds done under it and not the truths inscribed upon it that constituted the desecration?”

What the courts confirmed

Moyer posted a $500 bond on the desecration charge and was promptly re-arrested out of “military necessity.” Union lawyers sued for his release and a district judge ordered him freed; the military refused to comply.

The case went to the Colorado Supreme Court. The union argued there was no broad civil unrest to justify martial law in the first place — the courts in the strike counties were still sitting — and that everything flowing from the declaration was therefore unlawful. The state argued the governor held the authority to declare it, and that union members had put themselves outside the law’s protection. The state won. Peabody ordered Moyer released once the union prepared to appeal to the federal courts, apparently sensing the question was not one to keep testing.

A related case nonetheless reached the United States Supreme Court on a technicality, and the governor’s power to declare martial law was upheld again.

Afterward

The crackdown ended in one of the strangest footnotes in Colorado politics: the day the state had three governors. Union members campaigned hard for the Democrat Alva Adams, who favoured arbitration over military intervention; business backed Peabody; and with the state still under military law the Democrats ran on “Anybody but Peabody!”

The election was a shambles, with ballot-stuffing in Democratic Denver and vote suppression against miners in the mountains. Adams led on the count, the Republican legislature investigated, and — unsurprisingly — sided with Peabody, who was sworn in on condition that he resign at once and hand the office to his lieutenant governor.

By then the Western Federation of Miners had been badly weakened. Public opinion had turned hard against it after a man hired by the union bombed the Independence railway station, killing thirteen strikebreakers.
