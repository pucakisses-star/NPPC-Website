What American universities did to their own students and faculty between October 2023 and the end of 2024 has been counted, case by case, by ten different organizations, from the Crowd Counting Consortium and the National Lawyers Guild to PEN America, FIRE, Palestine Legal, and the AAUP. Their datasets overlap, cross-check, and agree on the shape of the year. Synthesized, the numbers look like this.

Arrests

Between 3,100 and 3,300 documented arrests at campus pro-Palestine actions from April 17, 2024, day one of the Columbia encampment, through mid-June. Columbia produced the largest single-institution count (282 at Hamilton Hall, plus the earlier sweeps), and the joint Columbia/CUNY operation on the night of April 30 was the largest single-night police mobilization on an American campus in decades. Counting from October 7, 2023, taking in the vigils, marches, and sit-ins before the encampments and the actions after them, total arrests passed 5,000 by year's end.

Faculty

More than 500 faculty members faced institutional discipline during the period: arrests, suspensions, tenure denials, terminations, canceled fellowships, non-renewed contracts. Some cases became national news: Caroline Fohlin, the Emory economist tackled on camera by state troopers; the Columbia suspensions arising from encampment-defense walkouts; the Florida public-university cases driven by open state political pressure. Most did not. The quiet non-renewals and withdrawn offers at private institutions produced no headlines, only ended careers.

Students

More than 2,000 students were suspended, evicted from campus housing, banned from campus, blocked from registering, refused diplomas, or expelled. These sanctions run through university disciplinary systems, not courts. There are no rules of evidence, no appointed counsel, no public record, and in many jurisdictions they proved harsher and far more durable than the criminal cases they accompanied. USC's removal of valedictorian Asna Tabassum's commencement speech, the Columbia and Barnard suspensions, and the UCLA post-clearance disciplinary letters are the visible edge of a much larger file.

Organizations, speakers, ceremonies

More than 50 chapters of Students for Justice in Palestine, Jewish Voice for Peace's campus network, and adjacent groups were suspended, derecognized, stripped of campus space, or dissolved outright, most aggressively in Tennessee, Florida, Texas, and Indiana, where state political pressure drove the decisions. Dozens of pro-Palestine speakers, including Palestinian-American academics holding named invitations, were disinvited. USC canceled its valedictory address; several universities shrank or canceled commencement entirely; multiple presidents, Columbia's Minouche Shafik among them, resigned as the disciplinary cycle collided with faculty no-confidence votes.

The federal layer

Above all of it sat the Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights, which opened dozens of Title VI investigations into universities over pro-Palestine protest. Whatever their formal purpose, the investigations functioned as federal pressure on administrators to escalate: a thumb on the scale that university counsel could cite whenever a harsher sanction needed justifying.

What the record shows

Taken together, the data describe the broadest institutional repression of campus dissent since the anti-Vietnam-War years of 1968–1972, carried out with striking uniformity across public and private institutions in every region. The arrests are the visible, trackable part. The disciplinary record, quieter, less countable, and harder to appeal, is the part that will still be shaping people's lives when the last criminal docket closes. NPPC tracks the cohort and publishes the names of defendants whose criminal cases remain open; the 2024 campus year is part of the contemporary U.S. political-prisoner record.
