An 86-year federal sentence for shots that hit no one. That is the arithmetic at the center of Aafia Siddiqui's case, and it is why her name is raised by Pakistani heads of state, U.N. bodies, and human-rights organizations more consistently than that of any other prisoner in the American federal system. In 2024, a renewed international clemency campaign pressed the Biden administration to act on it. The administration left office without doing so.

The disappearance and the trial

Siddiqui was an MIT-trained neuroscientist with a Brandeis PhD who had lived and worked in the United States with her three young children. In March 2003 she vanished in Karachi. For five years there was no official account of her whereabouts. Her family and an international advocacy network maintain — with corroboration assembled by journalists and U.N. investigators — that she spent some or all of those years in U.S. custody at Bagram Air Base, where detainees of that era have consistently described torture.

In July 2008 she surfaced in Ghazni, Afghanistan, in the custody of local police. During an interrogation session the next day, she was alleged to have grabbed a U.S. soldier's rifle and fired at the Americans and Afghans in the room. She wounded no one; she herself was shot in the abdomen. Flown to New York and tried in 2010 on attempted-murder and assault charges — never terrorism counts — she was convicted on all counts after a trial in which substantial portions of her proposed defense were excluded, and sentenced to 86 years.

Carswell

Siddiqui is held at the Federal Medical Center in Carswell, Texas, the Bureau of Prisons facility for medically complex women. Her lawyers and family have documented years of medical neglect and at least one serious assault — a July 2021 attack by another prisoner that counsel says the BOP never adequately investigated. Through 2024, the Muslim Legal Fund of America, CAIR, and her family pressed the administration for a humanitarian transfer to Pakistani custody, an arrangement Pakistan's government has formally requested more than once. Amnesty International, the Center for Constitutional Rights, and the U.N. Working Group on Arbitrary Detention have all called for her release over the years.

Her case carries one uniquely cruel complication. In January 2022, a British-Pakistani man took four hostages at a Texas synagogue while demanding her release; the FBI killed him and the hostages survived. Siddiqui had nothing to do with it — her family and lawyers condemned it immediately — but the episode fused her name to an antisemitic attack in national coverage and made the political cost of clemency, already high, higher still.

The question the sentence asks

Strip the case to what a court actually found: a woman, previously disappeared for five years, convicted of firing a rifle and hitting no one, sentenced to nearly nine decades. Around that finding sits everything a court never resolved — Bagram, the missing years, the excluded defense. President Biden's departure in January 2025 moved the file to an administration with no visible interest in it. NPPC lists Siddiqui on the U.S. political-prisoner roster because a sentence of that length, for conduct that injured no one, in a case built atop the rendition apparatus, is what political imprisonment looks like in a federal courtroom.
