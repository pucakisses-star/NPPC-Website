President Trump said Sunday night that news organizations reporting on the Iran war should face treason charges — a crime whose maximum penalty in the United States is death — escalating a week-long administration campaign against coverage of the conflict.

"Media outlets circulating 'fake news' should be brought up on Charges for TREASON for the dissemination of false information," Trump wrote on Truth Social. The post singled out The Wall Street Journal for reporting, sourced to two U.S. officials, that five Air Force refueling tankers were struck and damaged on the ground at Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia during an Iranian missile strike. The Journal reported the planes were damaged but not destroyed and were being repaired.

Trump called the story "false reporting" — and then, in the same post, confirmed its substance. Four of the tankers are "in service," he wrote, and one "will soon be flying the skies." Nothing in that account contradicts the Journal's. The president also complained about an AI-generated video that purported to show the USS Abraham Lincoln on fire, blaming "Corrupt Media Outlets" for its spread without naming any. AFP had already fact-checked the clip a week earlier and found it fabricated.

The FCC joins in

The treason post landed a day after Trump's Federal Communications Commission chair, Brendan Carr, threatened to pull the broadcast licenses of outlets he accused of "running hoaxes and news distortions." Carr offered no examples. Trump wrote that he was "thrilled to see" the threat, denouncing "Corrupt and Highly Unpatriotic" news organizations. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has spent the same stretch of days publicly attacking coverage of the war, now in its third week. Aboard Air Force One on Sunday, Trump dismissed a reporter as "a very obnoxious person" for asking why 5,000 additional Marines and sailors are being sent to the Middle East.

Senator Ed Markey of Massachusetts warned Carr in a letter Sunday that the administration is engaged in a "blatant attempt to muzzle the free press" whenever coverage of the war departs from the White House's preferred account. "Your Saturday post follows that same logic but extends it to the coverage of an active military conflict," Markey wrote, "where the chilling effect on journalists and the damage to the public's right to know are most severe."

Why it matters here

Treason is the only crime the Constitution defines, and the framers defined it narrowly — levying war against the United States or adhering to its enemies — precisely because English kings had used elastic treason law against critics. No American journalist has ever been convicted of treason for reporting. A president proposing capital charges over a story his own statement corroborates is not describing a legal case; he is describing a punishment for coverage. Paired with license threats from the broadcast regulator, it amounts to a coordinated message to newsrooms covering an active war: report what the government disputes, and the government will treat you as the enemy. That message — not any prosecution likely to survive a courtroom — is the mechanism, and its intended audience is every editor deciding tonight what tomorrow's front page can safely say.
